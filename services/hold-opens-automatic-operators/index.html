Two questions decide everything on this page. Does that door release when the alarm sounds? And can somebody who cannot pull hard get through it?

The first has a yes-or-no answer, and it is worth testing rather than assuming. A magnetic holder wired to the panel, or a closer with an integral electromagnetic hold-open, will let the door go on a signal and the closer will take it to a latched position. A wedge, a hook or a mechanical hold-open arm will not. From ten feet away a compliant hold-open and a non-compliant one look exactly alike, which is how the wrong one survives in a building for a decade.

The second is where low-energy push-plate operators come in — on entrances, accessible washrooms and suites. In a town that hosts the Abilities Centre, nobody needs persuading that these matter. What gets missed is what sits behind them: when an operator fails or the power drops, the door falls back to the closer, and that closer has usually been wound up over the years precisely because the motor was covering for it. We service holders, alarm interfaces, plates, wireless actuators, operator arms and control boxes — and we set the closer so the door is still usable by hand.

FAQs

Hold-Opens & Accessible Door Operators — your questions

What actually has to happen when the fire alarm sounds?

Every hold-open in the building has to release, and every door it was holding has to close and latch by itself. That is one test, and it is worth doing deliberately rather than assuming. We release the magnets, watch each door through its full swing, and note the ones that closed but did not latch — that second group is where the problems hide.

The push plate at our community centre works some days and not others. What is that?

Intermittent operation is nearly always the actuator or its power: a wireless plate on a fading battery, a loose connection at a hard-wired switch, or a receiver that has drifted. Occasionally it is the operator’s control board. It is diagnosable on site, and it is almost never a reason to replace the whole operator.

Is an automatic operator the same thing as an accessible entrance?

Not on its own. The operator is one part of it. The door still has to be usable manually, the approach has to work for someone in a chair, and the closer behind the operator has to sit within the opening force a barrier-free route allows. We handle the hardware end, and we will tell you plainly when the problem is not something a closer can solve.
