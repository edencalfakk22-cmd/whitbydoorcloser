Whitby gives a door closer two different problems six months apart. In February the hydraulic fluid is thick and the unit is slow, so the door reaches the strike without enough momentum to latch. In July the humidity off the lake swells the leaf and the gaskets and the same door starts binding in its frame. The instinctive fix for both is to wind the spring up — and the instinctive fix is what causes the third problem.

Exposure is the local variable on top of that. An entrance at Port Whitby or on Whitby Shores takes wind straight off the lake, wind that both holds a door open against its closer and slams it shut when it lets go, while an identical door in a sheltered plaza three kilometres inland needs nothing like the same settings. Treating a portfolio as one number is why the exposed openings are always the first to fail.

A round covers it. Each unit checked for fluid loss and for arm or bracket damage; sweep, latch and backcheck set where the unit allows; every rated door released from wide open to confirm it latches unaided; and opening force measured wherever the route has to stay barrier-free, because the Building Code limits that and a wound-up spring is the usual way the limit goes. You get a written list at the end — what was adjusted, what needs parts, and what will not make it through another winter.

FAQs

Seasonal Closer Adjustment & Latch Tuning — your questions

What actually changes between a September setting and a January one?

The fluid. It thickens as the temperature drops, so the same valve position gives a slower door, and slower doors run out of momentum before the latch engages. Doors and frames also move with the season, so a strike alignment that was fine in the heat can be a millimetre out in the cold. We set with both in mind rather than for the day we happen to be standing there.

Does wind off the lake really change how a door should be set?

It does, and the Port Whitby and Whitby Shores entrances prove it every winter. Wind both holds a door open and slams it shut, so those openings want a working backcheck and enough closing power to finish the swing — settings that would be far too aggressive on a sheltered interior door. Treating every opening the same is why the exposed ones are always the first to fail.

Can you leave us a list of what needs replacing next year?

Yes, and for most property managers it is the most useful part of the visit. Along with what we adjusted, you get the units that have lost fluid, the arms and brackets on their way out, and the doors where the frame or the strike is the real problem — so the work lands in a planned budget instead of a February emergency.
