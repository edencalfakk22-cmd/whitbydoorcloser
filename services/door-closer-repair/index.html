Three complaints cover nearly every closer call we take in Whitby, and they point at different parts. "It slams" is damping — the fluid, or a valve. "It stops before it shuts" is spring power, or fluid that has thickened past what the unit has left. "It fell off" is not the closer at all; it is what the closer was screwed into.

The last one is worth dwelling on, because it is the one that repeats. Closer arms are stronger than the thin hollow-metal frames they are usually anchored to. Somebody goes through the door with both hands full, the fasteners take a load they were never meant to see, and the whole assembly pulls out with the door apparently undamaged. Putting the same screws back into the same holes buys a few months. Reinforcement, through-bolts, or moving to a sound anchor point ends it.

On the older downtown and Brooklin storefronts there is a fourth situation: a perfectly good body carrying twenty years of mismatched arms, brackets and shims, on a door that has been re-hung more than once. That is a parts job and a re-hang, not a replacement, and it is worth somebody saying so. What is never a repair is a leaking body — commercial closers are sealed at manufacture, there is no fill port, and a unit with oil on the outside of it has already made the decision for you.

FAQs

Door Closer Repair, Rebuild & Replacement — your questions

The door dies about an inch short of the latch. What is that?

Latch speed, spring power, or a strike that has moved — and in a Whitby winter, often all three at once. Fluid thickens as it cools, so a unit set in September no longer has enough left at the end of the swing come January. We check whether the closer has the power for that door before touching the valves, because turning up a tired unit only buys a few weeks.

Can an old closer be rebuilt rather than replaced?

The body cannot — modern units are sealed and are not serviceable in the field. Everything around it can be: arms, forearms, shoes, brackets, covers and the fasteners holding it to a door that may have been re-hung more than once. On downtown storefronts we quite often find a sound body on ruined hardware, and that is a parts job rather than a replacement.

The entrance faces the lake and it is worse than anything else we own. Is that normal?

Entirely. Wind off Lake Ontario at Port Whitby and Whitby Shores pushes on a door hard enough to expose a closer that was coping fine three streets inland. Those openings usually want more spring power and a working backcheck to stop the wind flinging the door into the wall — and they need re-checking after the first real cold snap rather than being set once and forgotten.
